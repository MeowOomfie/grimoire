# You’ll Master Aim, Just Give Me 19 Minutes
channel: Shotty

## Covers
Aim fundamentals for gun players: where to look (target or crosshair), crosshair choice, sensitivity ranges, eye stretches and eye training, technique for clicking heroes versus tracking heroes, aim segmentation, smoothness then precision training, and off-game habits (session length, sleep, hydration, diet, caffeine timing, practice before bed, consistency). For any player who wants better weapon accuracy.

## Claims
- The coach holds above 65% weapon accuracy across his Deadlock games, which he says is top 1%. [0:00]
- Most players in every FPS have fairly bad aim, so below average effort (simple technique plus a little practice) gets you well above average aim. [0:31]
- Aim is hand-eye coordination, and most players never train the eye part, which is why fast targets seem to teleport when you track them with your eyes. [0:31]
- Like learning to drive, managing target, crosshair, ammo and abilities feels overwhelming at first and becomes easy after a month or two. [1:01]
- Beginner rule: when the target is close or moving fast, look at the target; when it is far or moving slowly, look at your crosshair. [2:03]
- Better players stare almost only at the target, because you can read their movement before it happens, for example which way a bounce padding Holliday will air strafe from her gun direction or shoulders. [2:33]
- Test: put your crosshair an inch from a bot and stare at the bot; you should see both the target and crosshair in focused vision, which lets you adjust. As you get better, stare at the target. [3:04]
- Crosshair rule: bigger and more cross shaped is for hit confirmation (finding the center), smaller and closer to a dot is for precision. [3:34]
- If you lose your crosshair while aiming, make it bigger; if you swear you were on target but missing, make it smaller or closer to a dot. [3:34]
- Constantly switching sensitivity slower and faster does not fix over or under aiming; most problems are lack of practice or poor technique, so pick one and practice with it. [4:05]
- The coach can play a random sensitivity at about 90% of his peak performance. [4:36]
- Recommended range at 1600 DPI: fastest 0.5, slowest 0.22; at 800 DPI double it, 0.44 to 1.0. Outside that range you get diminishing returns unless you are trained or have a specific situation. [5:08]
- If you get headaches or targets blur, do eye stretches; staring only at the center of the screen for hours fatigues the eyes. [5:39]
- Doing eye stretches in the morning before playing removed the coach's headaches and fatigue so he could play as long as he wanted. [6:41]
- Eye training (targets moving around the screen) teaches you to follow targets with only your eyes instead of moving your head or swinging your camera to look. [6:41]
- Even if you never use Kovaak's or Aim Labs, knowing technique puts you ahead of about 90% of players. [7:11]
- Technique depends on hero type: clicking heroes like Holliday with her single fire gun and Lady Geist, or tracking heroes like Vyper, Wraith, Haze and pretty much every gun carry. [7:42]
- On clicking heroes, do not hold the shoot button: the shot fires before you finish adjusting, you miss far more and keep reloading. [7:42]
- Holding fire while strafing makes you strafe off your target, then click again and again, playing cat and mouse with your own movement; tap fire instead. [8:42]
- Holding fire is fine only against huge targets like Mo & Krill or Bebop; against normal sized heroes like Vyper or Wraith, tap fire. [8:42]
- For clicking shots, do not move then click; get close to the target, micro adjust and track them for a second, then click. [9:12]
- Jumping players have a set arc, so at the peak of the jump they are a free shot. [9:43]
- Tracking heroes have the most aim problems because tracking is technique intensive. [9:43]
- The biggest tracking mistake is aim segmentation: jittering onto a strafing target in small stops instead of one smooth continuous motion, like slamming the brakes instead of easing off the gas. [10:13]
- Make every aim movement continuous; segmenting wastes movement and makes you feel inconsistent and out of control. [10:44]
- To aim better, aim less: every over-adjustment is one more chance to make a mistake. [11:15]
- Much of aiming, including tension management and not death gripping the mouse, is conscious effort plus a little practice until technique becomes second nature. [11:15]
- If you aim train, focus mostly on smoothness maps first to stop segmenting, then on precision tracking, which is what real aim looks like. [11:45]
- Precision tracking a strafing target in the air (the coach's Haze on a Vindicta) uses tiny fingertip motions; you cannot do tiny motions smoothly until you can do big ones, so smoothness first, then precision. [12:17]
- Aim training is like working out: past about an hour and 30 minutes of hard training you get diminishing returns, so train your weaknesses and then go play real games. [13:19]
- Sleep is about 95% of improvement, and the difference between 6 and 7 hours is gigantic. [14:19]
- Do not sit on your phone for 2 hours after waking or go straight to games; a little weightlifting or cardio before playing makes you much more alert and locked in. [14:50]
- Aim for 7 to 8 hours of sleep consistently; a week of bad sleep is fine, but three weeks to two months of it has big impacts. Judge sleep and hydration week by week. [15:21]
- Hydration has a massive impact on overall performance, so drink water. [15:52]
- Older players should not write themselves off: studies show a bad night's sleep hurts reaction time more than the gap between a 20 and a 60 year old. Dial in diet (whole foods, blueberries), hydration and sleep instead. [15:52]
- To avoid a caffeine crash, have no caffeine in the first 1.5 to 2 hours after waking, so morning adenosine clears first. [16:53]
- Stop caffeine about 12 hours before bed, for example around 1:00 pm if you sleep at midnight. [17:23]
- Practice closer to bed (a few hours before): the brain consolidates the most recent practice first during sleep, so aim or movement practiced at night is learned earlier in sleep. [17:54]
- Rest days matter more than working days, and consistency beats volume: 10 minutes five to seven days a week beats 2 hours a day that you quit after two weeks. [18:25]
- Just doing it is 95% of the battle. [18:25]
