# The Framework to be Top 1% in Any Game
channel: Deathy
date: 2026-09-14
url: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=o9-H6URkfDc

## Covers
A general improvement framework from a Deadlock coach, with Deadlock examples: mindset, the "loading phase" for a new game, a five-step improvement process (identify mistakes, execution vs game understanding, focused practice, trust the process, solidify learnings), regression, and teammates and matchmaking (the 40-40-20 rule, Elo hell, comms).
For players of any team game, Deadlock included, who want to climb with limited playing time.

## Claims
- Be critical of yourself and avoid excuses and external blame (teammates, matchmaking, "losers queue", draft, team comp); the only thing you can control is yourself. [1:33]
- Focus most of your attention on yourself, not your teammates, but don't blame yourself for everything; some games are out of your control. [2:34]
- You can't prevent emotions like frustration at a feeding teammate, but you can train how you react to them. [2:34]
- When a game makes you upset and seems out of your control, either focus on what you can do better or just play for fun for the rest of that game to protect your mental state. [3:34]
- Getting angry at people for playing poorly reduces your improvement over time; staying in a good mental state is a very important part of improving. [3:34]
- Chasing rank is bad: a long-term rank goal is fine, but if it is your only purpose every loss feels like a step back and creates more frustration. [4:06]
- Enjoy the process and have fun; if your only goal is a high rank you won't get very far. [5:06]
- Chase short-term goals to stay on track, but they should never involve ranks or wins (not "win 20 games today" or "rank up one rank today"). [5:06]
- To climb you must improve faster than the average player, since everyone improves just by playing; improving slowly can leave your rank flat or falling even though you are better. [5:36]
- With limited time (job, family, kids), make your playing time high quality so you climb without playing 40 hours a week. [6:06]
- People overrate communication in online games; mute liberally. The value of the average comm is far lower than the value of your mental state. [6:36]
- Mute anyone who is toxic, distracting, or telling you how to play when you think they are wrong; don't think twice about it. [7:06]
- Always be cordial to teammates and ask nicely from a working-together angle ("it'd be nice if we did this together", "I think we need to deal with X hero"); people don't like being told what to do. [7:06]
- Never share anything negative in voice chat; don't point out a teammate's 0 and 7 or that the game is probably lost. [8:07]
- Believe you can do it; self-defeat becomes a self-fulfilling prophecy, and nearly anyone watching has the potential to reach the top 1% of their favorite game. [8:37]
- The grind matters: an hour a week will never improve you fast enough to rank up; you need enough time to learn the mechanics. [9:39]
- In the loading phase of a new game, repetitions are a must: learn the mechanics, characters, abilities, guns and objectives, play as much as you can, have fun and don't overthink it. [9:39]
- Watching YouTube guides in the loading phase is fine, but you don't have to be overly strategic, and new friends don't need to be overly guided; people discover a lot themselves in the first hours. [10:40]
- Someone with two games played (his example: Ludwig in Deadlock) is basically uncoachable because they don't know the heroes' abilities or base mechanics yet. [10:40]
- The loading phase is about 5 to 10 hours for a simple game, but easily 50 to 100 hours for a complex MOBA like Deadlock, League or Dota. [11:11]
- During the loading phase, try things and find what you like; play around your strengths. [12:13]
- He no longer relies on mechanics, so he plays heroes with easier execution that leverage his game understanding. [12:44]
- Without characters (for example Counter-Strike), find the play style you like, such as entry fragger. [12:44]
- The five improvement steps: identify your mistakes, execution vs game understanding, focused practice, trust the process, solidify your learnings. Adapt them to your own learning style rather than copy-pasting. [13:44]
- Step 1: identifying your own mistakes is one of the hardest steps and the most common reason people get coaching. [13:44]
- The easiest accessible way to spot your mistakes is watching high-level players; VODs you can pause are better than live Twitch unless you want to ask them questions. [15:14]
- Be specific in what you watch: pick a high-level player with your play style or heroes (for example, if you mostly play Lash, watch games where he plays Lash). [15:45]
- Watch the parts of the game you struggle with, for example if you are lost in the Deadlock mid game, watch their mid game and the transition they make. [16:45]
- Biggest tip: before the player makes a decision, pause and ask what you would do and why (for example a Lash in lane at 8 minutes: go to the centers, push the wave further, or go kill someone), then play it and compare. [17:15]
- If the top player does what you predicted you probably understand it; if not, there may be something you missed or a new learning. [17:45]
- Top players also make mistakes, so don't treat something seen once as the right play; trust patterns. [18:16]
- Paid coaching is a luxury that saves time; anyone can reach top ranks without it. Advice from higher-ranked friends or community members is fine, even if you pick up some bad habits that can be unlearned later. [18:46]
- When asking a coach or higher-ranked friend, bring a thesis of what you think you're doing wrong and ask if they agree and why; it trains you to analyze your own mistakes. [19:17]
- Step 2: separate execution from game understanding. Execution is your ability to carry out a plan; in Deadlock it is aim, movement and ability usage (hitting abilities and using them at the right time), basically how good you are at fighting. [19:47]
- Execution transfers heavily between games (aim from Overwatch transfers to Deadlock and Counter-Strike), so people with many gaming hours can have a big execution edge over you. [20:47]
- Execution usually takes much longer to get good at than game understanding, which can come quickly if you are efficient. [21:47]
- You can isolate execution with aim trainers like KovaaK's or Aim Lab or movement maps, but many top players never aim train; playing the game is the biggest way to improve, and aim training is worth it if aim is really holding you back. [21:47]
- Know whether you are execution heavy or understanding heavy and play to that strength: with strong understanding and weak execution in a MOBA, pick a low-execution hero with easy spells that leverages game understanding. [22:49]
- In Counter-Strike with bad execution, don't entry frag; use utility, support teammates or become an IGL. If your execution is strong from other games, pick play styles that use it. [23:20]
- Step 3: focused practice. Just queuing improves you, but slowly; focused practice targets specific parts of your gameplay bit by bit. [23:50]
- Go into each session with one small goal that takes days or weeks, not months, and is measurable and achievable, a yes or no on whether you did it. [24:22]
- Counter-Strike goal examples: use all your utility every round, or always have a flash before you peek. [24:52]
- Deadlock goal example for pushing waves: aim for around 20,000 trooper damage at 20 minutes every game. [25:22]
- Deadlock timing goal examples: be on time for objectives, be on the side the Urn spawns at 10 minutes, be at the buffs when they spawn at 5 minutes, be at your small camp when it spawns at 2 minutes. [25:22]
- Practice goals should have nothing to do with winning or losing, so you can hit them either way; this helps learning and your mental, since a loss where you hit your goal feels less bad. [25:53]
- Build foundations first, like grammar when learning a language; abusing a specific strategy can give cheap wins but is probably bad for long-term growth. [26:24]
- Step 4: trust the process. Progression graphs zigzag up and down with an overall positive trend; don't panic. [26:55]
- There are walls between ranks (for example Plat 3 to Emerald 1): the game plays differently, things you learned lower may stop working, and an edge from knowing what others didn't disappears. [27:25]
- When you move to a new focus you often temporarily get worse at the previous one; that is usually where you derank, one step back for two steps forward. [28:26]
- Short deranking periods are very normal even while improving; matchmaking in team games causes variance, so anyone can get unlucky for five or 10 games, though you should still ask what you could do better. [28:57]
- Step 5: solidify your learnings so you don't forget them. A favorite way is explaining concepts to lower-ranked players, which also helps your fundamentals because you remember why people do things. [29:28]
- Keep a log of past objectives and learnings (Google Doc, Excel, any format) and occasionally review one of your games to check you are still doing them. [30:29]
- Regression: don't overthink it. A day or two of regression is nothing to worry about; sleep, exercise and focus all affect performance. [31:30]
- If you are still trending down after a couple of days, review your games to check you haven't stopped applying past learnings, and restart at step 1 if you have. [32:31]
- Meta and how the game is played often change by rank, so you may need to adjust your play style for a new bracket; while relearning you might rank down further than before, which he calls the Elo slingshot. [32:31]
- If you're stuck for a week or two, start again at step 1: identify mistakes, get help from higher-ranked people, and put your ego aside. [34:01]
- 40-40-20 rule (made up, but he explains the logic): in your correct rank bracket, 40% of games are unwinnable, 40% are unlosable if you play your average, and 20% are decided by your performance. [35:01]
- In your correct bracket a win rate above 60% over hundreds of games is nearly impossible; you can hold 70% for 20 to 50 games, but matchmaking's job is to push everyone to 50%. [36:01]
- You can't tell in a game which kind it is, so the rule doesn't excuse giving up; it means some losses are out of your control and you shouldn't be too down on yourself. [37:01]
- Elo hell isn't real in any remotely working matchmaking: if you don't belong in a bracket you will get out with enough games, though at two games a week you won't move much. [37:31]
- The common denominator in every game is you and whoever you queue with; if you lose a lot with friends, ask whether they are part of the problem. [38:32]
- If your friends constantly blame matchmaking and others, they are not the right people to climb with; sometimes you need different friends for improving. Queuing with self-critical friends who want to learn helped him a lot. [39:02]
- You are doing very well at 55% win rate or above; that is enough to improve and climb steadily, and aiming higher than 55% for longer than 20 to 30 games is unrealistic. [40:02]
- Don't spend energy on bad teammates: don't argue or keep trying to get uncooperative teammates to do what you want; mute them and move on. [40:32]
- Go into a game trusting your teammates; if some prove uncooperative, selectively stop trusting them and play around the teammates you have been having success with. [41:02]
- Bad comms are worse than no comms: limit communication to what's actually important, nothing negative, and don't make false calls like saying someone is one HP when they are near full. [41:33]
- When the whole team is chatting and having fun, that good morale is important and fine. [42:04]
